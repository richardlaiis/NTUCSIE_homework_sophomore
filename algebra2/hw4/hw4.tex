\begin{lemma}
Suppose $G$ is an finite abelian group, $x,y,\in G$, and $|x|=r$ and $|y|=s$. Then there exists an element of $G$ which has order $\mathrm{lcm}(r,s)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
First, suppose that $\gcd{(r,s)}=1$, then $\mathrm{lcm}(r,s)=\frac{rs}{\gcd{(rs)}}=rs$. Given that $|x|=r$ and $|y|=s$, consider $z=xy$, then $z^{rs}=x^{rs}y^{rs}=e$, we have $|z|\mid rs$ (so $|z|\le rs$). 

Let $|z|=m$, so $e=e^s=z^{ms}=x^{ms}y^{ms}=x^{ms}$, we have $r\mid ms$. Similarily, $e=e^r=z^{mr}=x^{mr}y^{mr}=y^{mr}$, we have $s\mid mr$. But $\gcd{(r,s)}=1$, so $r\mid m$ and $s\mid m$, the result implies $rs\mid m$. Hence, $|m|\ge rs$. But also $|m|\le rs$, so $|z|=m=rs=\mathrm{lcm}(r,s)$.

We now consider the general case, we can decompose $r$ as a product $r=r_1r_2r_3r_4$ as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item $r_1=$the product of all prime factors of $r$ which aren't prime factors of $s$.
    \item $r_2=$the product of all prime factors of $r$ which have equal power in $r$ and $s$.
    \item $r_3=$the product of all prime factors of $r$ which have higher power than those in $s$.
    \item $r_4=$the product of all prime factors of $r$ which have lower power than those in $s$.
\end{itemize}
Define $s=s_1s_2s_3s_4$ similarily, with $r$ and $s$ in exchanged places. And we have $\mathrm{lcm}(r,s)=r_1r_2r_3s_1s_3$ and $\gcd{(r_1r_2r_3, s_1s_3)}=1$. Now $|x^{r_4}|=r_1r_2r_3$ and $|y^{s_2s_4}|=s_1s_3$, if we consider the two elements: $x^{r_4}$ and $y^{s_2s_4}$, $|x^{r_4}y^{s_2s_4}|=r_1r_2r_3s_1s_3=\mathrm{lcm}(r,s)$ (trivially $x^{r_4}y^{s_2s_4}\in G$) by the first part of the proof. So there exists an element of $G$ which has order $\mathrm{lcm}(r,s)$.
\end{proof}

Let $E$ be $n$-dimensional extension of the finite field $K$ (Let $|K|=q$). Because $E$ acts like an $n$-dimensional vector space over $K$ (which has $q$ elements), the field $E$ must contain exactly $q^n$ elements.

The nonzero elements of $E$ form a multiplicative group $E^{\times}=E\backslash \{0\}$, it contains $q^n-1$ elements, i.e., $|E^{\times}|=q^n-1$.

By Lagrange's Theorem, the order of every element in $E^{\times}$ must divides the $|E^{\times}|$. Hence, if $\alpha\in E^{\times}$, we have $|\alpha|\mid (q^n-1)$, thus $\alpha^{q^n-1}=1\Rightarrow \alpha^{q^n-1}-1=0\Rightarrow \alpha^{q^n}-\alpha=0$.

Hence, every element of $E^{\times}$ is the root of $x^{q^n}-x=0$, even the zero satisfies it. We obtain that every element of $E$ is a root of $x^{q^n}-x=0$, also every polynimial of degree $q^n$ has at most $q^n$ distinct, which is equal to the order of $E$. Therefore, $E$ is the splitting field of $x^{q^n}-x$.

Since both $K_1$ and $K_2$ are the splitting field of the same polynimial ($x^{q^n}-x$), they are isomorphic.
