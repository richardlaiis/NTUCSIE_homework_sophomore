\subsection*{(a)}
By the tower law, $[K:\mathbb{R}]=[K:\mathbb{C}][\mathbb{C}:\mathbb{R}]=2^{a+1}m$. Let $L$ be the Galois closure of $K$ over $\mathbb{R}$. Set $G=\mathrm{Gal}(L/\mathbb{R})$, so $|G|=[L:\mathbb{R}]$. It's obvious that $|G|=2^bm'$ for some $b\le a, m'|m, \text{m' is odd}$ (also by tower law).

Let $P\le G$ be a sylow 2-subgroup of $G$, so $[G:P]=m'$ is odd. Let $F$ be the corresponding intermediate field, we have $[F:\mathbb{R}]=[G:P]=m'$. Suppose $\alpha\in F\backslash \mathbb{R}$, and its minimal irreducible polynomial has degree $d$ ($d\ge 2$). Clearly, $d|[F:\mathbb{R}]$ so $d$ is odd. But any odd-degree polynomial has a real root, which condradicts the irreducibility. Hence, $F=\mathbb{R}$ and $G=P$ is a 2-group.

Therefore, $[L:\mathbb{R}]=2^b$, by the tower law, $[L:\mathbb{R}]=[L:\mathbb{C}][\mathbb{C}:\mathbb{R}]=2[L:\mathbb{C}]$, $[L:\mathbb{C}]=2^{b-1}$. Since $[K:\mathbb{C}]$ divides $[L:\mathbb{C}]$, the only possibility is $a=0$.
\subsection*{(b)}
Assume, $a\le 1$.
Now define $H=\mathrm{Gal}(L/\mathbb{C})\le G$, the subgroup is not trivial since $|H|=[L:\mathbb{C}]=[L:K][\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{C}]\le 2^a\le 2$

Since $2$ is the least prime dividing $|H|$, there exists a normal subgroup of $H$ of index $2$, let it be $N$, $[H:N]=2$. By the Galois correspondance, the fixed field $E=L^N$ satisfies $[E:\mathbb{C}]=[H:N]=2$, so $E$ is a degree-2 extension of $\mathbb{C}$.

Pick any $\beta\in E\backslash \mathbb{C}$, its minimal irreducible polynomial over $\mathbb{C}$ must be quadratic:
\[x^2+bx+c, b,c\in \mathbb{C}\]
By the formula, the roots are $\frac{-b\pm \sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2}$, now $b^2-4ac\in \mathbb{C}$ and every complex number has its square root in $\mathbb{C}$ trivially, so $\beta\in \mathbb{C}$, a contradiction. 

Hence, $a=0$.

\subsection*{(b)}
\begin{lemma}
Splitting Fields are Normal Extensions.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If $f(x) \in K[x]$ and $L$ is the splitting field of $f(x)$ over $K$, then we can directly assume $L = K(a_1, \dots, a_n)$, where $a_1, \dots, a_n \in L$ are all the roots of $f(x)$. Since adjoining finitely many algebraic roots generates a finite-dimensional vector space over the base field, we know that $L/K$ is a finite extension. Next, we will prove that $L/K$ is a normal extension. If $\alpha \in L$ and its minimal polynomial over $K$ is $p(x)$, we need to show that all roots of $p(x)$ lie in $L$.

Let $\beta$ be another root of $p(x)$. Since $p(x)$ is an irreducible polynomial in $K[x]$ and $\alpha, \beta$ are its roots, we know there exists a $K$-isomorphism $\phi: K(\alpha) \to K(\beta)$. Since $f(x) \in K[x]$, we have $f(x) \in K(\alpha)[x]$. Furthermore, since $\phi$ fixes the elements of $K$, we obtain $f^\phi(x) = f(x)$. Now, considering $f(x)$ as a polynomial in $K(\alpha)[x]$, we know $K(\alpha)(a_1, \dots, a_n)$ is the splitting field of $f(x)$ over $K(\alpha)$. On the other hand, considering $f^\phi(x) = f(x)$ as a polynomial in $K(\beta)[x]$, we know $K(\beta)(a_1, \dots, a_n)$ is the splitting field of $f^\phi(x)$ over $K(\beta)$.

Because $K(\alpha)(a_1, \dots, a_n) = K(a_1, \dots, a_n)(\alpha) = L(\alpha)$ and $\alpha \in L$, we have $K(\alpha)(a_1, \dots, a_n) = L$. Similarly, we have $K(\beta)(a_1, \dots, a_n) = K(a_1, \dots, a_n)(\beta) = L(\beta)$. Rearranging these results: we have an isomorphism $\phi: K(\alpha) \to K(\beta)$ and a polynomial $f(x) \in K(\alpha)[x]$, and additionally, $L$ and $L(\beta)$ are the splitting fields of $f(x)$ and $f^\phi(x)$ over $K(\alpha)$ and $K(\beta)$, respectively. Because an isomorphism between base fields can always be extended to an isomorphism between their corresponding splitting fields, we know there exists an isomorphism $\sigma: L \to L(\beta)$ such that $\sigma|_{K(\alpha)} = \phi$.

Since $\phi$ fixes the elements of $K$, $\sigma$ also fixes the elements of $K$. That is to say, $\sigma$ is a $K$-isomorphism, meaning $L$ and $L(\beta)$ are isomorphic over $K$. Since isomorphic field extensions must have the same degree over their base field, we know $[L:K] = [L(\beta):K]$. Thus, from the multiplicativity of degrees in towers of extensions, $[L(\beta):K] = [L(\beta):L][L:K]$, we obtain $[L(\beta):L] = 1$. This implies $L(\beta) = L$, which means $\beta \in L$. We have proved that all roots of $p(x)$ must be in $L$, meaning that $p(x)$ splits over $L$; hence, by definition, $L/K$ is a normal extension.
\end{proof}

Since $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{2}, \zeta_8)/\mathbb{Q}$ contains all roots of $x^4-2$ ($\sqrt[4]{2}, -\sqrt[4]{2}, \zeta_8^2\sqrt[4]{2}, -\zeta_8^2\sqrt[4]{2}$), it's a splitting field for the irreducible polynomial (in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$) $x^4-2$. Every splitting field is normal, so $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{2}, \zeta_8)/\mathbb{Q}$ is normal.

Clearly, $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{2}, \zeta_8)=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{2}, i)$. By tower law, $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{2}), i:\mathbb{Q}]=[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{2}, i):\mathbb{Q}(i)][\mathbb{Q}(i):\mathbb{Q}]=4\times 2=8$. Hence, the degree for the extension is $8$.

Let $E/K$ be an arbitrary finite extension. If $E=K$, then $[E:K]=1=p^0$, so we may assume that $E\ne K$.

Because $K$ has characteristic 0, every algebraic extension of $K$ is separable, therefore, $E/K$ is a finite separable extension.

In field theory, the \textbf{primitive element theorem} states that every finite separable field extension is simple, i.e. generated by a single element. So, there exists a Galois closure $N$ of $E$ over $K$. (Since we can write $E=K(\alpha)$ for some $\alpha$ as a finite extension over $K$, and $\alpha$ is algebraic over $K$, there exits a unique irreducible polynomial $f$ over $K$, and then by adjointing all roots of $f$, we can construct the splitting field $N$, which is normal and separable.)

Let $G=\mathrm{Gal}(N/K)$ be the galois group, by definition $|G|=[N:K]=p^am$ for some positive integer $a$ and $m$ ($p\nmid m$). Assuming the case $m$ is greater than $1$. By Sylow's first theorem, there exists a Sylow p-subgroup $P$ with order $|P|=p^a$. By the fundamental theorem of Galois theory, there's a corresponding intermediate fixed field $L=N^P$ such that $K\subseteq L \subseteq N$.

We have $[L:K]=[G:P]=\frac{|G|}{|P|}=\frac{p^am}{p^a}=m$, since we assume $m>1,p\nmid m$ there's a contradiction.

Hence, $|G|=[N:K]=p^a$. By the tower law, $[N:K]=[N:E][E:K]$ and $[E:K]>1$, $[E:K]$ must be some power of $p$, completes the proof.

Let $G=\mathrm{L/K}$ be the Galois group, by definition $|G|=[L:K]=p^m$.

We claim that there exists a sequence of subgroups $G=H_0\supsetneq H_1\supsetneq \dots\supsetneq H_m=\{e\}$ such that for every $1\le i\le m$:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $H_i\triangleleft G$
    \item $[H_{i-1}:H_i]=p$
\end{enumerate}
Let's prove the claim by induction. For the base case ($m=1$), $|G|=p$, by lagrange theorem $H_m$ must be trivial, and the two statements are satisfied.

Assume the lemman holds for all p-group of order $p^{m-1}$ ($m>1$), because $G$ is non-trivial p-group, by class equation:
$$|G|=|Z(G)|+\sum{[G:C_G(x_i)]}$$
where $x_i$'s belong to distinct conjugacy classes and not in $Z(G)$, since the terms within the sum are all some power of $p$, so is $|G|$, we know $p\mid|Z(G)|$, the center of $G$ is not trivial.

By Cauchy's theorem, $Z(G)$ contains an element of order $p$, which can generate a cyclic group $N$ of order $p$, and obviously $N\triangleleft G$.

Consider the quotient group $G/N$, which has order $p^{m-1}$. By our inductive hypothesis, $G/N$ contains a chain of normal subgroups:
$$G/N=\overline{H_0}\supsetneq \overline{H_1}\supsetneq\ldots\supsetneq \overline{H_{m-1}}=\{e\}$$
such that each $\overline{H_i}\triangleleft G/N$ and $[\overline{H_{i-1}}:\overline{H_{i}}]=p$.

By the fourth isomorphism theorem (correspondance theorem), each quotient corresponds uniquely to a subgroup of $H_i$ of $G$ containing $N$. Because $\overline{H_i}$ is normal in $G/N$, its corresponding preimage $H_i$ is normal in $G$. Furthermore, the index is preserved, $[H_{i-1}:H_i]=[\overline{H_{i-1}}:\overline{H_i}]$. The mapping details can be seen \href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Correspondence_theorem}{here}. By some observation, we know $H_{m-1}=N$, set $H_{m}={e_G}$, we have $[H_{m-1}:H_m]=p$, completes our induction. \textbf{The claim is proved so perfectly!}  

Now define $K_i$ to be the fixed field of $H_i$, by the Galois correspondance we have the chain:
$$K=K_0\subsetneq K_1\subsetneq\ldots K_m=L$$
$K_i/K$ is Galois if and only if $\mathrm{Gal}(K_i/K)\triangleleft G$, since our lemma explicitly construct each $H_i\triangleleft G$, so the second statement is True.

Using tower law for the extensions $K_{i-1}\subsetneq K_{i}\subseteq L$, we have:
$$[L:K_{i-1}]=[L:K_{i}][K_{i}:K_{i-1}]$$

So, $|H_{i-1}|=|H_i|[K_{i}:K_{i-1}]$. We have $[K_{i}:K_{i-1}]=[H_{i-1}:H_i]=p$, completing the proof.
